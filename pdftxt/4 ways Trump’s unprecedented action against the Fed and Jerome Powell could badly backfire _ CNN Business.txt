New York — President Donald Trump wants interest rates to fall dramatically, the stock
market to boom and, perhaps most of all, for his handpicked Fed chair to get oﬀ the stage.
But the Trump administration’s unprecedented criminal investigation into Jerome Powell
may not achieve any of these outcomes.
In fact, the shocking escalation of the long-running Trump-Powell feud could end up
backﬁring. It could delay rate cuts, fuel jitters on Wall Street and persuade Powell to stick
around long after his term as chair expires in May.

The Justice Department’s threat to criminally indict Powell may even make it harder for
Trump to replace Powell with a loyalist.
“This is lawfare at its worst,” former Dallas Fed president Richard Fisher told CNN on
Monday in a phone interview. “I ﬁnd it hard to believe they would stoop to this level. This is a
step too far.”
Fisher, who sits on the board of directors at CNN parent Warner Bros. Discovery, said he
doesn’t know what Trump is trying to accomplish through the criminal probe “other than
being vindictive.”
‘Trump isn’t going to win’
In many ways, a red line has been crossed.
To avoid freaking out investors and CEOs, the Trump administration in the past had avoided
going directly after Powell, resorting to insults in public statements instead.
Investors voiced their immediate displeasure with the dramatic escalation, brieﬂy resuming
the “sell America” trade that started last spring in response to Trump’s historic tariﬀ hikes.
US stocks and the dollar retreated Monday morning, though only modestly.Advertisement

Economists told CNN they worry the DOJ criminal investigation of Powell takes direct aim at
the independence of the Fed, which is designed to be insulated from political interference.
“Attempting to criminalize the conduct of monetary policy is an outrage,” University of
Michigan economics professor Justin Wolfers told CNN. “Every American should oppose this.
Bad economics. Bad politics. Bad for the rule of law. Bad for markets.”

Tim Mahedy, a former oﬃcial at the San Francisco Fed, said Trump is trying to send a
message to the current and future Fed oﬃcials who don’t get in line with Trump’s low-rate
desires.
“Trump isn’t going to win this,” said Mahedy, CEO and chief economist at Access Macro, a
consulting company. “Powell isn’t one to be pushed around. They’re not going to cut rates in
January.”
The move even irked Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, who told people that he is unhappy
with the decision to prosecute Jerome Powell, a source with knowledge told CNN. Bessent has
expressed concern that the decision will hurt markets – something has warned Trump in the
past that ﬁring Powell could result in.
Rate cut odds remain slim
Indeed, the market sees just a 5% chance of a Fed rate cut at the next meeting in late
January, according to the CME FedWatch Tool. That’s little changed from 4.4% on Sunday and
down from 17% a week ago.
“This is an outrageous act which, from any other president, would be a shocker,” former Fed
vice chair Alan Blinder told CNN in an email. “Fortunately, the Fed and its chair, Jerome
Powell, are not easily intimidated.”Advertisement

about a rate cut reluctant to be seen as doing the president’s bidding.
“Overall, this is counterproductive to the president’s agenda and almost forces the FOMC to
be more hawkish to shirk political pressure,” Mahedy said.

The White House has sought to distance Trump from the DOJ probe.
White House Press Secretary Karoline Leavitt told reporters on Monday that Trump never
directed the DOJ to open an investigation into Powell, whom the president has previously
derided as a “dumb guy,” a “numbskull” and an “absolute fool.”
Powell may not ride off into the sunset
Trump’s eﬀorts to oust Powell could cause the opposite to happen.
While Powell’s term as chair expires in May, in theory he could stick around at the Fed for
years because his term at the powerful board of governors doesn’t end until January 2028.
Powell has been coy about whether he would stay at the Fed, denying Trump a replacement
on the board and ensuring a strong voice in favor of Fed independence remains.
When asked by CNN about this idea in December, Powell punted by saying: “I’m focused on

What’s telling, however, is that as of last week, bettors on prediction market Kalshi an 85%
chance that Powell is out as Fed governor before August. But those odds plunged after news
of the criminal investigation and they now sit at just 55%.
In other words, the market thinks Powell is more, not less, likely to stick around – exactly the
opposite of what Trump wants.
“This strikes me as a really unwise and bone-headed move,” said Douglas Holtz-Eakin,
president of the American Action Forum, a center-right think tank. “This is really stupid.”

‘Really unwise’
Trump could soon announce his pick to replace Powell, with leading contenders including
former Fed Governor Kevin Warsh and White House economist Kevin Hassett.
However, that person must get conﬁrmed by the US Senate. The Powell investigation appears
to have already complicated that task signiﬁcantly.
Minutes after Powell’s deﬁant video statement Sunday night, Republican Sen. Thom Tillis
said on X this subpoena should remove “any remaining doubt” that Trump oﬃcials are
“actively pushing to end the independence of the Fed.”
Tillis, who sits on the Senate Banking Committee that will consider a nominee to replace
Powell, said he will oppose the conﬁrmation of “any nominee for the Fed – including the
upcoming Fed Chair vacancy” until the Powell legal matter is resolved.
Alaska Republican Sen. Lisa Murkowski said Monday that she supported Tillis’ move, calling
the Trump administration’s action “nothing more than an attempt at coercion.”
If other Republicans follow suit, that could slow or derail Trump’s eﬀorts to get a Powell
replacement conﬁrmed.
